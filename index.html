Systems, and what measuring them actually showed.

AeroRAG — hybrid retrieval over civil aviation regulation

Code Live demo Preprint

Retrieval and question answering over 43 DGCA and ICAO regulatory documents: 10,572 chunks, 368 of them derived from tables. Sparse and dense retrieval run in parallel, are merged by Reciprocal Rank Fusion, rescored by a cross-encoder, and answered with citation-enforced prompting. There is no retrieval framework underneath it, which is what makes each stage independently ablatable.

PDF ingestion → structure-preserving chunking → BM25 Okapi + ChromaDB bi-encoder → RRF fusion (k=60) → ms-marco-MiniLM cross-encoder → citation-enforced generation
Four-way ablation · 80-question benchmark stratified by evidence type
ConfigurationRecall@5MRR@10
Dense only0.690.660
RRF fused, no reranking0.760.655
Full pipeline0.800.725
BM25 + reranking0.800.730

Two findings were worth more than the system itself. Reranking earns its keep on ordering rather than coverage: it moves MRR@10 from 0.655 to 0.725 while Recall@5 moves only within sampling noise. And BM25 with the same reranker matched the full pipeline, which turned out to be a limitation of the benchmark rather than a result about retrieval, because synthetic questions reuse their source wording and never properly test the dense branch.

The decisive stage was ingestion, not retrieval architecture. Standard PDF extraction flattens tables into prose, so no table reached the index as a table. After rebuilding ingestion to preserve them, table-evidence queries still retrieve at 0.675 Recall@5 against 0.925 for prose (p = 0.010), and 0.500 against 0.875 under dense retrieval alone (p = 0.0006). Neither fusion nor reranking closes that gap. Since 21% of the corpus pages carry no text layer at all, a benchmark built from the index cannot see what ingestion discarded, which is the argument for reporting a coverage audit alongside recall.

Deployed at roughly 2.3 s median end-to-end latency: 120 ms retrieval, 400 ms fusion and reranking, 1.8 s generation. Code, indices and the evaluation set are released under MIT.

Background

How I got to retrieval.

I trained as a mechanical engineer and came to machine learning through the problem rather than the other way round. Four years of working with aviation regulatory documents at AAI is what made retrieval interesting to me: the corpus is large, the language is unforgiving, and being approximately right is not useful to anyone.

The engineering habits transferred better than I expected. Sizing an actuator against a measured load and sizing a reranker against a measured latency budget are the same discipline. You establish what the system actually has to do, then prove that it does it.

Earlier, at BIT Mesra, I led the pneumatics subsystem on the Formula Student team, raised ₹11 lakh in sponsorship across two campaigns, and trained roughly 40 students a year on vehicle design, fabrication and competition documentation.
